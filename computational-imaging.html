Letting the algorithm see: computation as the new lens

July 28, 2026 · Computational Imaging

For over a century the recipe for a picture was fixed: light enters a lens, the lens forms an image, a sensor records it. Computational imaging asks a subversive question — what if we let the lens be imperfect on purpose, capture something messier, and let an algorithm reconstruct the picture we actually wanted? Suddenly the boundary between optics and software dissolves, and "resolution" becomes something you can recover rather than only something you can buy with bigger glass.

Breaking the diffraction limit

The diffraction limit says a lens can't resolve features smaller than about half a wavelength. Super-resolution microscopy — STED, SIM, PALM/STORM — gets around this by coding the acquisition (special illumination, controlled photo-switching) so that the computation afterward can separate what the optics alone couldn't. The physics hasn't changed; the measurement strategy has. That's the whole spirit of the field: move the cleverness out of the glass and into the math.

An ordinary lens captures light. A computational system captures information — and information can be reconstructed even when the image can't.

Bioimaging and inverse problems

In bioimaging, this matters enormously: living tissue scatters and blurs, and you can't just add a bigger objective. Computational methods treat imaging as an inverse problem — given the blurry, noisy measurement and a model of how light traveled, recover the underlying object. My interest here is the bridge: I come from precision metrology (knowing errors precisely) and nonlinear optics (new on-chip light sources), and computational imaging is where those tools become useful for actual life-science questions — seeing structures cells hide from conventional microscopes.

Why it's my connector

If metasurfaces are the reimagined hardware and nonlinear optics is the reimagined light source, computational imaging is the reimagined output — the step that turns a clever optical system into a picture a biologist can use. It's the direction that most explicitly ties my other work together, which is exactly why I listed it as a core research interest rather than a side note.
