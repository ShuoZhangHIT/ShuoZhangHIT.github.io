I rebuilt a picture from a blurry grid

February 14, 2026 · Computational Imaging

Computational imaging has a reputation for being abstract, but the first time it landed for me was very concrete: I had a stack of low-resolution images, each taken through a cheap microscope objective with a slightly shifted field of view, and I fed them into a Fourier ptychography loop. A few hundred iterations later, the screen showed a picture sharper and wider than any single frame could possibly have held. No better lens. Just better math, eating the blur for breakfast.

That reconstruction is the day I understood "the algorithm is the lens."

Stitching in frequency, not pixels

The trick is that each low-NA image captures a different patch of the sample's Fourier spectrum. Overlap them correctly and you assemble a spectrum that reaches far higher spatial frequencies than any one shot resolved — then invert it to get a high-resolution, large-field-of-view image. The "ptychography" part is the redundancy: the overlapping views let the algorithm recover both the image and the aberrations, simultaneously, by iterative phase retrieval.

A conventional microscope is limited by the glass at the front. A computational one is limited by how cleverly you can write the reconstruction — and that limit moves every year.

Watching the ghostly initial guess sharpen, iteration by iteration, felt like watching a photograph develop in reverse: first noise, then edges, then a clean, wide, detailed field. The computation wasn't polishing a picture. It was creating information that no single exposure contained.

Why this is my favorite frontier

Computational imaging is where optics admits it can't win alone and invites the computer to the table — super-resolution past the diffraction limit, denoising of live-cell data, learned optics that co-design the hardware and the solver. My contributions to the Nature Methods denoising work live in exactly this spirit: letting algorithms repair what physics alone would leave noisy.

If you want the longer arc of this idea, the Research section has the super-resolution and FPM pipelines I keep tinkering with. But the core thrill hasn't changed since that afternoon: a blurry grid in, a sharp world out.
