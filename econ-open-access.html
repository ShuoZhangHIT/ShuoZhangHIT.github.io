The hidden market behind every PDF I download

December 14, 2025 · Economics of Knowledge

I've downloaded hundreds of papers over the past two years. Most through university subscriptions, some through Sci-Hub, a few directly from authors' websites. Each download feels free. It isn't. Behind every PDF there's a market — publishers charging libraries thousands per journal, authors paying APCs to make their work open-access, readers locked out when their institution can't afford the subscription.

This note is my attempt to map that invisible market, as someone who lives inside it every day.

Who pays?

In traditional publishing: libraries pay subscriptions, universities pay libraries, tuition and grants pay universities, taxpayers and students pay ultimately. In open-access: authors (or their grants) pay article processing charges that can run $2,000–$11,000 per paper. Either way, money changes hands. The only question is whose hand it leaves.

The current system optimizes for publisher revenue, not for the speed or breadth of knowledge dissemination. Those two goals have diverged sharply.

What it means for a student researcher

Practically, it means my access to papers depends entirely on which institution I'm affiliated with. If I weren't at HIT, half the papers I cite would be behind paywalls I couldn't afford. That's not just inconvenient — it's a structural barrier that favors researchers at wealthy institutions and disadvantages everyone else.

It also means that when I eventually publish, I'll face a choice: put my work behind a paywall where it reaches fewer readers but costs me nothing, or pay an APC I may not be able to afford so anyone can read it. Neither option feels right.

The direction I'm watching

Preprint servers (arXiv, bioRxiv) are already bypassing much of this. Plan S in Europe and growing funder mandates are pushing toward universal open access. The question isn't whether the system will change — it's whether the new system will replace rent-seeking with something fairer, or simply relocate the payment point.

I don't have answers. But as someone who both consumes and produces scientific knowledge, I think paying attention to the economics of that process is part of being a responsible researcher.
