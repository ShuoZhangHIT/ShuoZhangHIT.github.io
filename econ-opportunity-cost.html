The opportunity cost of every hour I spend in the lab

June 20, 2024 · Economics & Decision-Making

I took an introductory microeconomics course in my first semester mostly to fill a general-education requirement. I expected supply-and-demand curves and indifference maps — useful, dry, forgettable. What I didn't expect was that one concept would reframe how I think about my own time as an undergraduate researcher: opportunity cost.

Every hour has a price tag

Opportunity cost is simple: the value of the next-best alternative you give up when you make a choice. When I spend four hours debugging a Python script for my interferometry simulation, the opportunity cost isn't zero just because I wasn't "paying" anything. It's whatever else I could have done with those four hours — read a paper, write up notes, sleep, or just let my brain rest.

Economics doesn't tell you what to choose. It forces you to admit that you are always choosing something against something else.

This sounds obvious written out. But in practice, I spent most of my first year making choices by inertia — staying late because everyone else stayed late, tweaking a plot that was already good enough because it felt productive. Opportunity cost thinking interrupted that habit.

Not everything valuable can be measured

The trap, of course, is reducing every hour to a utilitarian calculation. Some lab time isn't "efficient" but it's where you learn things no syllabus covers: how to talk to a professor when your experiment failed, how to help a teammate who's stuck, how to recognize when you're too tired to think clearly.

So I don't literally calculate the opportunity cost of every hour. But I do ask myself one question before committing to something: "Is this the best use of this block of time, given what I'm trying to become?" Sometimes the answer is yes even when it looks unproductive on paper.

What economics gave a physics student

A vocabulary for trade-offs. A reminder that resources (time, attention, energy) are scarce and allocation matters. And a healthy suspicion of anything that feels like "free." Nothing in research is free — you're always paying with something. The question is whether you're paying consciously or by accident.
