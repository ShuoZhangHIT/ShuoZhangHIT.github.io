Where optics is going: my read on the next decade

July 22, 2026 · The field, from a student's seat

Predicting an industry is a losing game, and I am still a student with more questions than answers. But the optics I read in 2026 does not look like the optics in the textbooks on my shelf — and the direction of that drift is, to me, the most exciting part of being in the field right now. Here is how I see it reshaping itself.

From "better lenses" to "smarter reconstruction"

For a century, progress meant grinding a cleaner surface or coating a thinner film. That frontier is still alive, but a second one has opened beside it: computational imaging. Once you accept that the camera can recover what the optics threw away, the lens becomes a sampler and the algorithm becomes the instrument. Super-resolution microscopy and computational bioimaging are where this pays off first — and it is exactly the direction I am pulling my own work toward.

The next generational leap in imaging may not be a better piece of glass. It may be a better way to ask the glass what it saw.

Flat optics goes mainstream

Metasurfaces — arrays of sub-wavelength scatterers we design atom by atom — have spent a decade as a conference curiosity. What changed is fabrication maturity. As nanomanufacturing gets cheaper and more uniform, the promise of a lens you print instead of polish edges from demo to product: lighter AR visors, thinner phone cameras, lab-on-a-chip sensors. The physics I studied in the "sky is blue" post is quietly becoming a supply-chain item.

Photonics meets the chip

Silicon photonics has been "five years away" for twenty years, and this time the tailwind is real: AI datacenters are hitting the power and bandwidth wall that only light can move past. Co-packaged optics and photonic interconnects are no longer a research flex — they are an infrastructure necessity. For a student, that means nonlinear optics and on-chip devices stop being esoteric and start being employable.

AI-native optics

The most surprising shift is methodological. Inverse design — let an optimizer decide the geometry of a grating or a metalens — used to be exotic; now it is a default. My own quasi-phase-matching work leans on exactly this. The researcher's job is migrating from "derive the shape" to "specify the goal and curate the search." That is a different, and I think kinder, skill set: equal parts physical intuition and taste.

What I am betting on

If I had to put my undergraduate hours where my optimism is, it would be at the seam between these threads: computational imaging built on metasurface hardware, validated by nonlinear and adaptive-optics tooling. That intersection is still small enough that a student can matter in it, and large enough that it will still exist — and matter — when I finish a PhD.

This is a student's read, not a forecast. In ten years some of it will look naive. That is fine; the point of being early is to be wrong cheaply and learn fast.
