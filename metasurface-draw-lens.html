Drawing a lens with math

May 9, 2025 · Metasurfaces & Micro/Nano Optics

For most of my optics education, a lens was something you chose: you picked a curvature, a glass, a coating, from a catalog of things other people had figured out how to make. A metalens breaks that habit completely. There is no catalog. You decide, pixel by pixel, what phase each tiny pillar imparts to the light — and then you ask an optimizer to find the arrangement that bends the world the way you want.

My first inverse-design run felt less like engineering and more like collaborating with a stranger who sees in a language I don't.

Meta-atoms are the alphabet

The building block of a metasurface is a meta-atom: a subwavelength pillar of dielectric, whose height and radius set how much optical phase it delays. String enough of them together on a grid and, locally, they act like a continuous gradient-index surface. Tune that gradient and you get focusing, beam-steering, even achromatic behavior — all from a flat slab a few hundred nanometers thick.

The old question was "what lens can I buy?" The metasurface question is "what lens can I describe?" Description, it turns out, is design.

Inverse design flips the workflow. Instead of deriving a shape analytically, you state a target — focus here, at this bandwidth — and let a gradient-based optimizer, guided by electromagnetic simulation, push millions of little pillars toward it. My intuition picked the starting guess. The optimizer did the rest, and half the time I couldn't have drawn its solution by hand.

Why this corner hooked me

Micro and nano optics sits exactly where my two loves overlap: the crisp physics of wave propagation and the messy creativity of computation. A metalens is a poem written in Maxwell's equations and solved by a laptop. That combination — rigorous and open-ended at once — is why I keep coming back to it.

The Research section has the inverse-design notebook and the unit-cell phase library where I'm still teaching myself this craft, one simulation at a time.
