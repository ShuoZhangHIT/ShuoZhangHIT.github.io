Flat magic: how a sheet of nano-pillars could replace a box of lenses

July 28, 2026 · Metasurfaces & Micro/Nano Optics

For two hundred years, shaping light meant shaping glass: grinding curves, stacking elements, accepting that a camera or a microscope is mostly a carefully arranged pile of lenses. Metasurfaces propose something almost rude in its simplicity: what if a flat sheet of tiny patterned pillars, each smaller than the wavelength of light, could do the whole job?

From a lens to a library of scatterers

A conventional lens works by slowing light more in the middle than at the edges, so rays accumulate different phase and converge. A metasurface does the same thing with meta-atoms — sub-wavelength structures whose shape, size, and orientation we design so that each one imparts a precise, tailored phase shift. String enough of them together with the right pattern and you get focusing, deflection, holography, or polarization control, all in a layer thinner than a hair.

A metasurface isn't a smaller lens. It's a reprogrammable law of refraction, written point by point.

The interesting problem: inverse design

The physics of a single meta-atom is tractable. The interesting difficulty is the inverse problem: given a target optical function — "focus here," "beam-shape like that" — what arrangement of millions of pillars achieves it, and can we actually fabricate it? This is where optics meets optimization, machine learning, and nanomanufacturing tolerances. My curiosity here is less "can we make one cool element" and more "can we design robust flat optics that survive real fabrication, not just simulation."

Why it matters

If metasurfaces mature, chunky optical assemblies shrink toward flat chips: lighterAR/VR eyewear, simpler satellite imagers, lab-on-a-chip sensors. For someone who also works in precision metrology and computational imaging, metasurfaces are a natural bridge — they are the hardware that a computational reconstruction can lean on, and the thing whose performance a careful measurement can certify.

This is still more of a fascination than a pile of publications for me (my metasurface-flavored work so far lives mostly in the stray-light and structural-color angles), but it's a direction I'm actively growing into. The Research page tracks where it goes next.
