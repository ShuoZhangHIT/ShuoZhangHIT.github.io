Seeing the invisible: how we measure a nanometer and a microradian

July 28, 2026 · Ultra-precision Measurement

Most measurements we meet in daily life are generous: weigh yourself, and ±100 g doesn't matter; check the room, and a centimeter is nobody's business. Precision metrology lives at the other end of the spectrum, where the questions are "how far did that mirror move?" down to a fraction of a nanometer, and "by what angle did the beam tilt?" down to a microradian — about the width of a human hair seen from ten meters away.

This is the world I work in, and what first pulled me into optics. It is also, I think, the quiet foundation under almost every other glamorous thing we do with light.

Two workhorses: the autocollimator and the interferometer

An autocollimator measures tiny angles. A collimated beam leaves the instrument, reflects off a target mirror, and returns; any tilt of that mirror doubles into a shift of the returning spot, which we read optically. It is conceptually simple and astonishingly sensitive — microradian angles become easy. An interferometer measures displacement instead, by counting the light-and-dark fringes that appear when two beams recombine; each fringe corresponds to half a wavelength of motion, so a stabilized laser turns distance into a fringe count.

Together they let us watch things move that we literally cannot see move.

The real enemy: systematic error

Here is the part that hooked me more than the pretty sensitivity numbers. A measurement is only as true as its systematic errors allow. In an autocollimator, for example, tiny imperfections in the collimator objective — astigmatism,coma, a slightly off-axis beam — bend the returning ray in ways that masquerade as angle. My first-author paper in Sensors (MDPI) was exactly this: I modeled how lens systematic errors couple into the angle reading, both theoretically and experimentally, and showed how to predict and compensate them.

Resolution tells you how finely you can read a number. Systematic error tells you how wrong that number might quietly be. The second one is the harder fight.

That distinction — between seeing a small change and knowing its true value — is the heart of this field. A stray reflection, a thermal drift, an assembly misalignment: each one is a small liar, and metrology is the art of catching the liars.

Why it matters beyond the lab bench

Ultra-precision measurement is the substrate beneath semiconductor lithography, gravitational-wave detectors, telescope pointing, and precision manufacturing. The reason a chip can be printed at nanometer scale is, in large part, that someone, somewhere, could measure at that scale first. My patent (a single-beam three-degree-of-freedom method combining heterodyne interferometry and autocollimation) is a small attempt to push that envelope — measuring displacement and two angles from one beam.
