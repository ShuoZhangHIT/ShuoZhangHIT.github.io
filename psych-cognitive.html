Why a physics student started reading cognitive psychology

March 15, 2024 · Psychology & Learning

I picked up Daniel Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow during my first semester at HIT, expecting a casual read between optics problem sets. What I found was something closer to a user manual for my own brain — and it changed how I study, how I do research, and how I notice when I'm about to make a mistake.

Physics trains you to think about systems outside yourself. Cognitive psychology forces you to think about the system that is doing the thinking.

System 1 vs. the lab bench

Kahneman's distinction between fast, intuitive System 1 and slow, deliberate System 2 maps surprisingly well onto lab work. When I'm aligning an interferometer, my hands move on instinct — that's System 1, pattern recognition built from repetition. But when I need to decide whether a fringe shift is real or thermal drift, I have to engage System 2: slow down, write down the alternative explanations, actually do the math.

The dangerous moments in research are not when you don't know enough. They're when your intuition feels so confident that you skip the check.

I've started treating that confidence as a warning sign. If an answer feels too obvious, I now pause and ask: is this System 1 being helpful, or is it substituting a plausible story for evidence?

Attention as a finite resource

The other idea that landed hard was attention as a genuinely limited resource — not a metaphor, but a measurable bottleneck. In a long coding session or an afternoon of data analysis, every context switch costs real cognitive cycles. I used to pride myself on multitasking; now I schedule deep-work blocks where I close everything except the one thing I'm working on.

The result isn't dramatic, but it's consistent: fewer bugs in code, fewer misread graphs, and a strange calm that comes from not fighting your own limits.

What this has to do with optics

Nothing directly. Everything indirectly. The best scientists I've met are not just technically sharp — they're metacognitively sharp. They know when they're tired, when they're biased, when they need to sleep on a result. Cognitive psychology gave me vocabulary for those states, and vocabulary is the first step toward managing them.
