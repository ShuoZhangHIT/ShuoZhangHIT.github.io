The Dunning-Kruger curve showed up in my lab notebook

December 8, 2024 · Psychology & Self-Awareness

You know the graph: confidence starts high when you know almost nothing, crashes as you realize how much you don't know, then climbs slowly as genuine competence builds. I always thought it was a cute internet meme. Then I plotted my own emotional trajectory across my first year of undergraduate research, and the shape was unmistakable.

Peak "I understand everything"

Month one of my first project: I had read three review papers, written a short simulation, and felt like I basically understood precision metrology. My lab notebook from that week is painful to reread — confident claims, hand-wavy arguments, zero awareness of what I was glossing over. That was the left side of the curve: high confidence, low competence.

The Dunning-Kruger effect isn't about being dumb. It's about not having enough expertise to recognize what expertise looks like.

The valley

The crash came when I tried to reproduce a published result and got numbers that made no sense. Not slightly off — wildly, embarrassingly wrong. I spent two weeks convinced there was a bug in the reference paper before a senior grad student sat me down and walked through the systematic error model I had completely ignored.

That afternoon my self-assessed competence plummeted. And honestly? It was the most useful thing that happened to me all year. The valley of despair is also the valley of learning.

Slow climb

Now, a year later, I can look at an autocollimator error budget and spot the missing terms. I can tell when a simulation is hiding behind pretty plots. My confidence is higher than it was in the valley, but it's a different flavor — less "I've got this" and more "I know which parts I've got and which ones still scare me."

If you're in the valley right now: good. Stay there. The climb out of it is where the real growth happens. Check the Research section if you want to see some of what I've been climbing toward.
