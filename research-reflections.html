Notes on doing research as an undergraduate — what I wish I knew sooner

July 8, 2026 · Research, honestly

Two years into undergraduate research, I have written exactly one paper that survived peer review and four that are still waiting to. I have broken more simulations than I have finished, and I have spent whole weeks on an idea that a senior PhD student dismantled in a five-minute conversation. If I could hand my first-year self a note, this would be it.

1. Own the failure, don't hide it

The instinct as a beginner is to present only the parts that worked. I learned the hard way that the fastest path through a dead end is to say out loud "this doesn't work, and here's what I tried." Advisors and labmates are not judges; they are the people who have already hit the wall you are currently staring at. Showing the failure invites the shortcut.

The result that didn't work is still a result — it just narrows the search space for everyone after you.

2. Read before you build

My first real project was an attempt to "improve" a measurement method I barely understood. I spent a month reinventing a control scheme that three papers had already covered. Now my rule is simple: for every hour I plan to spend coding or building, I owe the literature at least twenty minutes first. Reading is not a delay to the real work — it is the real work's head start.

3. Slow progress is still progress

Optics experiments are humbling. An autocollimator drifts. A sample is misaligned by two microns and the whole curve shifts. There were weeks where the only honest update I could give was "I confirmed it fails the same way three times." That is data. Consistency of failure is the first sign you actually understand the system enough to fix it.

4. Write the paper as you go

I used to treat writing as the final chore, something to survive after the science was "done." Now I keep a running document from week one — figures, half-sentences, the occasional paragraph I'll never use. When the submission deadline appears, I am assembling, not authoring. The paper you start early is the paper you actually finish.

5. Stay a little wide

It is tempting, as an undergraduate, to go narrow and deep to look serious. But the ideas that helped me most came from adjacent rooms — a stray-light trick borrowed from adaptive optics, a reconstruction trick from computational imaging that fixed a metrology problem. The fields on my site (measurement, adaptive optics, metasurfaces, nonlinear optics, computational imaging) are not a résumé; they are a network I let myself wander in.

None of this is profound. It is just the unglamorous machinery behind the tidy author list on the publications page. If you are an undergraduate somewhere staring at a simulation that won't converge: keep going. The bench teaches slower than the lecture, but it teaches truer.
