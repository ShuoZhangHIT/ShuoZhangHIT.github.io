What a point guard taught me about latency

October 5, 2025 · Sports · Basketball

The best point guard I ever played with never seemed to react. He just acted — a pass would already be on its way before the defender committed, a shot would rise before the help arrived. Watching him, I kept thinking about feedback loops, and then I realized he was basically a beautifully tuned control system running on instinct.

Here's the engineering analogy I couldn't shake.

Latency kills the loop

In any control system, the time between sensing and acting is dead weight. The longer your latency, the more you have to over-correct, and the more unstable you become. A point guard who only reacts to what he sees right now is always a half-step late. The great ones compress that loop by predicting: they don't wait for the defense to form, they anticipate the shape it's about to take. That's feed-forward control — acting on a model of the future instead of waiting for measurement.

Reaction is slow. Prediction is fast. The difference between a good playmaker and a great one is mostly the length of their internal feedback loop.

Reading the defense = reading a signal

A scrambling defense is noisy data. A settled defense is a clean pattern. What a veteran sees in a glance is the same thing an algorithm does in a filter — separate the meaningful structure (a help defender cheating toward the paint) from the noise (a wing flailing his arms). The pass that threads two defenders isn't lucky; it's a low-latency read of a high-signal situation.

Why it matters off the court

The same loop shows up in the lab: a wavefront sensor measures distortion, a controller decides a correction, a deformable mirror acts. Every extra millisecond of latency is contrast you'll never get back. Watching basketball didn't teach me adaptive optics — but it gave me a clean, intuitive picture of why latency is the enemy of every closed loop I'll ever build.
