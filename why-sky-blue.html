Why is the sky blue? From Rayleigh scattering to metasurfaces

March 12, 2026 · Optics, explained simply

"Why is the sky blue?" is probably the first physics question most of us ever asked. The textbook answer — "Rayleigh scattering" — is correct, but it hides a beautiful idea that, years later, connects directly to the metasurfaces I now study in the lab. Let's walk from the playground question to the research bench.

The short answer: small things scatter short waves

Sunlight is white, meaning it contains every visible color. As it passes through the atmosphere, it encounters molecules (N₂, O₂) far smaller than the wavelength of visible light. Lord Rayleigh showed that the scattering strength of such tiny particles scales roughly as 1 / λ⁴ — the inverse fourth power of wavelength.

Blue light has a wavelength about 60% that of red light, so it gets scattered roughly (1/0.6)⁴ ≈ 7–8× more strongly. Blue is therefore thrown in every direction across the sky, while red travels through relatively straight. Looking up, you see the scattered blue; looking at the sun, you see what's left — which at sunset, through a long, dense path of air, has had most of its blue removed, leaving red and orange.

The same sun, the same air — only the path length and the wavelength decide the color.

The twist: what if we designed the scatterer?

Here is where it gets interesting for an optics researcher. In the sky, the "scatterers" are accidental — random molecules doing physics whether we like it or not. But nature also makes color on purpose: the iridescent blue of a Morpho butterfly wing is not pigment at all. It is structure — nanoscale ridges that interfere with light. We call this structural color, and it is exactly the territory of metasurfaces.

A metasurface is a flat array of sub-wavelength building blocks ("meta-atoms") whose shape, size, and arrangement we engineer down to a few tens of nanometers. Instead of hoping molecules scatter the right way, we prescribe it: which wavelength bends, which reflects, which focuses. The same wave physics that paints the noon sky becomes a design knob.

Two ends of one idea

So the question "why is the sky blue?" and the question "can we build a flat lens that paints its own color?" are really the same question asked twice — once about what nature happened to give us, and once about what we choose to make.

That, to me, is the appeal of micro/nano optics: it turns explanation into invention. We understand the scattering, then we redesign the scatterer. The sky was the first textbook; the metasurface is the sequel we get to write.
